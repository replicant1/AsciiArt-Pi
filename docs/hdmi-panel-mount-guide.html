Capturing a mini-HDMI socket in a printed shell

Instead of buying a panel-mount connector that doesn’t exist in Type C, buy a short mini-HDMI extension and let the enclosure hold it. Three sections: why it’s worth doing, what the geometry has to be, and how the two shell halves close around it.

Where the force ends up

The mini-HDMI socket on a Zero 2 is surface-mount — a handful of solder tabs holding it to the board. Every time someone pushes a plug into the enclosure, that force has to be absorbed somewhere. Left to itself, it travels down the cable and into those tabs.

The two stops do opposite jobs

Pushing a plug in drives the socket inward, so it is the stop behind the connector that resists insertion. Pulling the cable drags the socket outward, and that is caught by the lip at the aperture — formed simply by making the hole smaller than the connector body. You need both, and they are at opposite ends of the part.

Why the shell has to split through the pocket

A connector this shape cannot be threaded in from either end once the pocket exists — it is wider than the aperture in front and wider than the cable gap behind. So the pocket has to open. Put the parting line through its centre and each half carries half the pocket; the connector drops into the lower half and the upper half traps it as the box closes.

Numbers to start from

FDM figures, for a 0.4 mm nozzle. Tighten the clearances for resin. Measure the connector with calipers rather than trusting a listing — overmoulded boots vary far more than the connector standard does.

Anchor to Pi ≤ 30 mm of free cable Bounds how much leverage a side load can apply to the Pi’s own socket.
Service loop 20–30 mm of slack A taut cable transmits everything. Slack between the anchor and the Pi is doing real work.

What bites

  1. Print orientation decides whether the lip survives. If the panel face is printed flat on the bed, extraction load pulls the lip apart across layer lines, which is the weak direction. Print that face vertical, or fillet the lip root so the load isn’t concentrated at a layer boundary.
  2. Check the connector has a flat back face. Some female extension ends are a fat plastic boot with nothing square for the rear stop to bear on. That kills the design. Buy two and pick the better one — they’re a few pounds each.
  3. Side loads still get through. The capture handles push and pull along the axis. A sideways yank levers the connector in the pocket and can crack the ribs. That’s what the cable anchor is for; it is not optional.
  4. Dry-fit on a test coupon. Print a 20 mm slab with just the aperture and pocket in it, ten minutes a go. Two iterations of that beat one wasted shell.
  5. No screening from the box. A metal connector in a plastic shell means the HDMI shield is grounded only through the cable. That’s fine at these lengths — just don’t expect the enclosure to contribute.

The part to buy

Any short mini-HDMI female to mini-HDMI male extension — this 0.16 m one is the right shape. The female end becomes the panel socket; the male end plugs into the Pi. Everything above is about the plastic around it.
