Panel connectors and controls

Three things have to cross the enclosure wall: video out, power in, and some way to shut the machine down. Each has a different answer, only one of them is genuinely hard, and the one that looks like an afterthought — the button — turns out to be free.

What crosses the wall

The Pi’s three exposed sockets are not equally worth exposing, and two of them are not exposed as themselves.

Nothing here needs the PiSugar 3, which is out of the picture for now. The choices below are made so that adding it later costs a cable and one wire, not a re-printed panel — noted where it matters.

Video out — mini-HDMI

Capturing the socket in the shell

A mini-HDMI (Type C) female panel-mount connector is not a stocked part. Full-size Type A has flanged sockets, bulkhead couplers, keystones and pre-made panel leads from a dozen suppliers; for Type C there is no equivalent, and the listings that claim to be one all mean mini plug on the cable end, full-size socket on the panel. Custom assemblies exist to order, with an MOQ in the dozens.

So buy a short extension and let the enclosure be the panel mount. The connector’s own body is captured between two features of the printed shell, and the Pi’s fragile surface-mount socket never sees a plug force again.

The two stops do opposite jobs

Pushing a plug in drives the socket inward, so it is the stop behind the connector that resists insertion. Pulling the cable drags the socket outward, and that is caught by the lip at the aperture — formed simply by making the hole smaller than the connector body. You need both, and they are at opposite ends of the part.

Why the shell has to split through the pocket

A connector this shape cannot be threaded in from either end once the pocket exists — it is wider than the aperture in front and wider than the cable gap behind. So the pocket has to open. Put the parting line through its centre and each half carries half the pocket; the connector drops into the lower half and the upper half traps it as the box closes.

Numbers to start from

FDM figures, for a 0.4 mm nozzle. Tighten the clearances for resin. Measure the connector with calipers rather than trusting a listing — overmoulded boots vary far more than the connector standard does.

What bites on the HDMI side

  1. Print orientation decides whether the lip survives. If the panel face is printed flat on the bed, extraction load pulls the lip apart across layer lines, which is the weak direction. Print that face vertical, or fillet the lip root so the load isn’t concentrated at a layer boundary.
  2. Check the connector has a flat back face. Some female extension ends are a fat plastic boot with nothing square for the rear stop to bear on. That kills the design. Buy two and pick the better one — they’re a few pounds each.
  3. Side loads still get through. The capture handles push and pull along the axis. A sideways yank levers the connector in the pocket and can crack the ribs. That’s what the cable anchor is for; it is not optional.
  4. Dry-fit on a test coupon. Print a 20 mm slab with just the aperture and pocket in it, ten minutes a go. Two iterations of that beat one wasted shell.
  5. No screening from the box. A metal connector in a plastic shell means the HDMI shield is grounded only through the cable. That’s fine at these lengths — just don’t expect the enclosure to contribute.

If the enclosure has room, the simpler alternative is a full-size panel-mount lead (Adafruit ADA978, flanged socket on a 40 cm cable) plus the official Raspberry Pi mini-HDMI-to-HDMI-female cable adapter — the flexible one, not the rigid dongle. That is one more junction, but everything is stocked and the mounting dimensions are published.

Power in — micro-B, exposed as USB-C

One part does the whole job

Unlike mini-HDMI, this is a solved commodity. Adafruit ADA4056, “Panel Mount Cable USB C to Micro B Male, 30 cm”: a USB-C socket with M3 mounting ears at 20 mm spacing on one end, a micro-B plug on the other. Panels up to 6.35 mm thick, 4.5 mm cable, screws included. There is no printed capture to design — it bolts to the panel.

The interesting decision is not the part, it is the connector on the outside face.

Why USB-C on the panel of a micro-B device

The cutout is the irreversible part. A cable can be swapped in a minute; a printed panel cannot be un-cut. USB-C is the cutout that survives changing your mind — including the specific change of mind already on the table, since the PiSugar 3 charges over USB-C. Fit it now and adding the battery later costs a different internal lead and nothing else. Put micro-B on the panel and that decision re-prints the panel.

The handled connector should be the durable one. Micro-B is the most-touched and least durable connector in the build. Its failure mode isn’t a clean break either — contacts wear, resistance creeps up, and it shows as intermittent undervoltage that looks like a software fault. Mating the micro-B once during assembly and never touching it again removes that whole failure class.

Where the volts go

This is a power problem, not a signal problem. No impedance control, no EDID, nothing to get subtly wrong — just 5 V and ground, and a budget. The Pi’s PMIC flags undervoltage at about 4.63 V, and every conductor and every mated pair between the charger and the board spends some of the difference.

Two things follow from the shape of that graph. The charger cable dominates, so a good supply and a short lead to it matter more than anything inside the box. And the gauge of the panel lead is the one number worth checking on the listing:

Power conductorResistanceDrop over a 300 mm lead at 1 A
24 AWG0.084 Ω/m~50 mV

300 mm of lead is 600 mm of round-trip conductor. ADA4056 states 24 AWG on the power pair and 28 AWG on the data pair, which is the right way round and is the reason to prefer it over an unspecified generic cable — those are commonly 28 AWG throughout and don’t say so.

The USB-C trap, stated plainly

A USB-C source only enables VBUS when it sees 5.1 kΩ from CC1 and CC2 to ground. Adafruit are explicit that ADA4056 has no CC pull-down resistors. So:

  • A-to-C cable from an ordinary charger — works.
  • C-to-C cable from a laptop or modern charger — dead. No power at all, no diagnostic, nothing.

There is nowhere to add the resistors on a moulded panel cable. So either commit to an A-to-C cable and label the box, or use a USB-C panel breakout board with accessible pads and fit the two 5.1 kΩ yourself. Worth deciding once, because the same applies to whatever eventually feeds the PiSugar’s USB-C port.

Supply

With no battery you are straight off the wall. The official Zero 2 W spec is 5.1 V / 2.5 A; this build draws well under 1 A with camera, SPI panel and HDMI all running. The headroom isn’t for the current, it is for keeping the left-hand end of that graph as high as possible.

Control — the off switch

You no longer have one, and the fix is free

Without the PiSugar there is no power button anywhere in the build, and pulling the plug on a running Pi is how SD cards get corrupted. Raspberry Pi OS solves this in one line. In /boot/firmware/config.txt:

dtoverlay=gpio-shutdown

The default is GPIO 3, active-low, internal pull-up. Wire a momentary button between GPIO 3 (header pin 5) and GND (pin 6) and you get both halves of a power button:

  • Press while running → clean shutdown.
  • Press again while halted, power still applied → the machine boots.

That is the behaviour a PiSugar’s power button gives you, for the price of a $2 switch and no battery module.

Noise is the failure mode, not the wiring

You are extending a high-impedance input feeding a hold-time detector, past an SPI panel clocking at MHz and a camera ribbon. A spurious long “press” shuts the machine down mid-frame, which is a genuinely confusing fault to chase.

  • 100 nF ceramic across the pair at the Pi end. Single most effective thing available; it shunts induced noise and debounces the switch for free.
  • Twisted pair — signal with its own ground return, not two loose hookup wires.
  • Short, and crossing rather than parallel. Under ~150 mm, and cross the SPI and camera cables at right angles.
  • 100–330 Ω in series in the signal leg if the switch has a metal body, as ESD protection for a connector a human touches.

There is a pleasant accident in that first item. Switching a logic-level line at microamps is dry-circuit territory, where cheap silver contacts can build an oxide film that never breaks down. The 100 nF charges through the pull-up between presses and dumps into the contacts on closure — exactly the wetting pulse that keeps the surfaces clean. The part that’s there for noise solves the contact problem too. If the button ever needs two or three presses after months idle, that is the mechanism, and a larger cap is the fix.

Which switch

SparkFun COM-11996, Momentary Button — Panel Mount (Black), AU$2.15 from Core Electronics; also in red as COM-11992. SPST normally-open, ¼″ threaded bushing with a nut, solder lugs, about 27 mm overall including leads. Three reasons it suits this build specifically: the nut takes finger force into the panel rather than into your solder joints, the solder lugs match thin twisted pair where spade terminals would not, and the plastic body simply does not offer the ESD path that a metal anti-vandal button does.

Buying rules, in order of how often they are got wrong:

  1. Momentary, not latching. Latching anti-vandal switches look identical in photographs and are frequently the default variant on a listing. A latching switch here misbehaves rather than fails cleanly.
  2. Ignore the current rating entirely. You are switching microamps at logic level. A 5 A waterproof switch buys nothing but bulk and a 16 mm hole.
  3. Check the depth behind the panel before finalising the CAD. It is the dimension suppliers routinely omit, and a 12 mm anti-vandal switch is typically 25–30 mm deep plus terminals.

The pin decision to make now

GPIO 2 and 3 are the I²C bus, and the PiSugar 3 talks to the Pi over I²C on those exact pins. If the battery goes in later, GPIO 3 is taken.

You can move the button with dtoverlay=gpio-shutdown,gpio_pin=5, but note what does and does not survive the move: shutdown works on any GPIO; wake-from-halt is a hardware property of GPIO 3 specifically. Move the pin and the button becomes shutdown-only.

If you do move it, GPIO 5 is the one, by the same reasoning already applied to the encoder’s SW pin: this chip defaults GPIO 0–8 to pull-up and 9–27 to pull-down, so on GPIO 5 the switch idles high from power-on rather than reading as held down until the overlay configures it. GPIO 5 is also clear of everything in use — SPI0, the panel’s 25/27/18, and the encoder’s 19/26/6.

Use GPIO 3 now. Wake-from-halt is worth having, the PiSugar is hypothetical, and if it does arrive the box is coming open for the battery anyway — moving one wire costs nothing at that point.

If the PiSugar 3 goes in later

What changes, and what doesn’t

  • The panel cutout survives. This is the whole reason for choosing USB-C now. Swap the internal lead from C-panel-to-micro-B for C-panel-to-C, into the PiSugar’s charge port.
  • Power moves to the PiSugar’s port, and must. Power arriving at the Pi’s own micro-B runs the Pi but does not charge the battery — you would have a panel socket that looks like a charge port and isn’t.
  • The shutdown button moves to GPIO 5 and loses wake-from-halt, because GPIO 3 becomes the PiSugar’s I²C.
  • The PiSugar’s own power button can take over the power-on job — and it can be brought to the panel without desoldering anything. Its button is a plain contact closure, so a second momentary switch wired in parallel across the existing one gives you both, reversibly. Desoldering a small SMD tact switch with an iron is the classic way to lift a pad, and PiSugar document no alternative connection point for the 3, so a lifted pad is unrecoverable.
  • Verify the two nets with a meter first. Continuity across the unpressed button reads open; with the board powered, one side is ground and the other sits at the pull-up rail and drops to 0 V while held. The ON / CTRL / OFF pad instructions found online are for the PiSugar 2 / S, which used a three-pin slide switch, and do not transfer to the 3.

The enclosure

The ribbon decides the layout

Start here, because it constrains everything after it. The Camera Module’s flex ribbon leaves the Pi’s short edge travelling parallel to its long edge. So if the Pi lies across the box and the camera looks out of the front, the ribbon has to turn through 90° in a few millimetres — which flex cable does not enjoy and should not be asked to do.

Turn the Pi instead. With its long axis running front to back, the CSI port faces the front wall and the ribbon reaches the camera dead straight, with one easy bend for slack and nothing tighter. That single rotation then decides two more things: the Pi’s port edge now faces the right wall, so that is where the HDMI and USB-C pockets go, and the back wall is left empty.

The result is a sloped console, 92 × 105 mm, 25 mm tall at the front and 62 at the back — a 19° face, comfortable to read a 2.4″ panel from a seated position and shallow enough that the box does not want to tip when you turn the knob.

The parting plane is the whole design

One horizontal plane at z = 25 mm does four jobs at once. It is the height of the front wall, so the base is a simple tray. It cuts the HDMI and USB-C pockets exactly in half, which is the only way to get a connector into a printed pocket at all. It clears the Pi and HAT stack. And it puts every hand-crimped joint in the base, where you can reach them with the lid off.

Where everything ended up, and why

  • Camera and shutdown button, front wall. The camera because the panel is a viewfinder for the scene in front of it; the button because that is the face you are looking at, and because keeping it in the base means one fewer harness crossing the parting line.
  • HDMI and USB-C, right wall. Not a style choice — it is where the Pi’s port edge ended up once the ribbon dictated the rotation.
  • Vents, left wall. Thin slots low down, mirrored on the right. Convection needs an inlet and an outlet; a sealed wedge with a Zero 2 rendering at 29% of a core will throttle before it errors.
  • Back wall, empty. A consequence rather than a plan, and a useful one: it is the natural place for anything added later.

What this layout costs

  1. The lid carries two live harnesses. Panel and encoder both mount to the sloped face, so opening the box lifts them. Leave 25–30 mm of slack in each and use silicone-insulated wire, which the build guide already recommends for exactly this case.
  2. 23 mm of port-edge clearance is not generous. Measure your actual plug bodies before committing — moulded boots vary far more than the connectors do, and this is the dimension that will bite.
  3. SPI still wants to be short. The 8-way harness runs from the HAT up to a panel on the lid, the longest run in the build. Keep MOSI and SCK under 10 cm; if it misbehaves, --lcd-spi-hz 20000000 is the escape hatch, not a rewire.
  4. Anchor the ribbon, not just the cables. The build guide asks for a clamp about a centimetre back from the CSI connector so flexing happens in the free span. Straight is not the same as unstressed.
