Character displays for the Pi, sourced in Australia

Five ways to put the ASCII matrix on a screen with a vintage character, ranked for a buyer in Sydney — where freight, not price, decides what is worth having.

The briefwhy this is not a monitor question

The app is a curses program whose picture is built from character cells. So the useful measure of a display is not its resolution but how many legible characters it can hold — and, for anything driven over a serial line, how fast those characters can be pushed down the wire.

Every option below is shown rendering the same scene through this project's own ramp,  .:-=+*#%@, at that display's real character grid. The difference between 80×24 and 42×8 is the whole argument.

Watch the aspect trap. A character cell is not square, and how far from square depends on the font. Terminal cells run about 2:1; a 6×8 pixel font is 1.33:1. So a 42×16 grid on a 6×8 font is a 2:1 picture, not a 4:3 one, and a 4:3 camera frame cannot fill it — you either pillarbox the image or crop the sensor. Each swatch below shows the cropped version, with the percentage of the frame that survives.

There is a shortcut hiding in that. The font sets how many characters you get, but the crop depends only on the panel's own shape:

frame kept = camera aspect (4:3) ÷ panel aspect

Which is why the 256×64 panels give away two thirds of the picture, and why a plain 4:3 panel gives away nothing at all. That single ratio explains the order of this list better than resolution does.

The deciding constraint

Sourcing rule

Anything with a CRT, buy locally with pickup. Anything flat, import or use an Australian distributor.

A VT220 or Wyse terminal weighs 10–18 kg and contains a CRT. Freighting one from the United States to Sydney runs A$300–600 plus real breakage risk — routinely more than the terminal itself. Flat modules, by contrast, ship for A$20–40. That single fact reorders the entire list: the cheap local CRT beats the better terminal you cannot economically get here.

Serial bandwidththe wire, not the Pi, is the limit

A full ASCII redraw costs roughly W×H bytes plus about 8 per row of cursor addressing, at 10 bits per byte. That puts a hard ceiling on frame rate for any serial terminal:

Achievable frame rate by grid size and line speed
Grid Bytes/frame 19,200 baud 38,400 115,200
80 × 24 ~2,100 0.9 fps 1.8 fps 5.5 fps
40 × 12 ~580 3.3 fps 6.6 fps 20 fps

A DEC VT220 tops out at 19,200 baud, so a full 80×24 picture is a slideshow at under one frame per second. Only the VT510 reaches 115,200, and that is what buys watchable motion at full size.

Green (P1) or amber (P3) phosphor, bought off Gumtree or Marketplace in Sydney. These are frequently free locally precisely because nobody wants to freight them, and there are active vintage collectors in the Hills district trading this gear.

Best module, no crop

320 × 240 monochrome graphic LCD

The one panel on this page whose shape matches the camera. 320×240 is 4:3, so nothing is cropped and nothing is pillarboxed — the entire field of view lands on the glass. At a 6×8 font that is 53×30, or 1,590 character cells: more than double the Noritake, and 83% of a full VT220 screen.

It is also the only module here you can read comfortably. These are typically 4.7–5.7″, so around 70–85 ppi — a character cell is roughly 2 × 2.9 mm, legible at arm's length. Every OLED on this page runs 121–172 ppi, where you need your nose against the glass.

The period look is physics rather than styling. These are STN panels — super-twisted nematic, where the liquid crystal twists roughly 180–270° between the polarisers instead of the 90° of plain TN. That steeper twist sharpens the response enough to drive a 320×240 matrix with no transistor per pixel, and it also leaves the cell birefringent, so it tints. The characteristic yellow-green and deep blue are a by-product of the optics, not a design decision, which is why they are so hard to fake. Both flavours are shown below: blue-negative, white pixels against a deep blue ground, and grey-green positive, dark pixels on a pale reflective one — the face of 1990s test equipment. (A compensating film gives FSTN, which neutralises the tint back towards black-on-white; you will see it in the part listings.)

The cost is wiring, and it is steeper than it first looks. Most use an 8-bit parallel interface (T6963C or RA8835), occupying a dozen or more GPIO pins — thirteen is typical: eight for the data bus, plus four or five control lines for write, read, chip enable, command/data and reset. The panel is 5 V logic against the Pi's 3.3 V GPIO, so level shifting is needed as well.

The supplies are what catch people out. The reference project below runs its panel from three rails: +5 V logic, −15 V for the LCD bias (generated from the +5 V with a TL497), and 75 V AC for a CCFL backlight, or a +12 V LED alternative. Current-production modules from Winstar and Newhaven generally integrate the negative-voltage generator and use LED backlighting — but check the datasheet rather than assume, because the difference between the two is a weekend and a project.

There is a working reference. Rasp-T6963C drives exactly this class of panel from a Pi, and contains a trick worth stealing: an HC595 shift register generates the eight data lines from three GPIOs, cutting the pin count well below thirteen. It also hard-wires whatever it can — /RD to +5 V, /WR to ground, reset through an RC network.

If that is more project than you want, a 320×240 ILI9341 SPI TFT has the same resolution and the same 4:3 shape — so the same 53×30 grid and the same 100% of frame — over five wires, for around A$20, with a mature Python library that takes a PIL image straight from the app. The catch is that these are 2.4–3.2″ colour TFTs at 125–170 ppi: you keep this entry's grid and lose its legibility and its STN character, which were two of the four reasons it ranks here. Driving it from userspace also sidesteps the drm/tiny kernel driver, where ILI9341 in particular still has initialisation trouble.

Wiring it takes eight female-to-female DuPont jumpers — both the Pi header and the module present male pins. The assignment below matches luma.lcd's defaults, so the library needs no reconfiguring:

Only the SPI0 lines — CS, MOSI, SCK — are fixed by hardware. RESET, DC and the backlight are ordinary GPIOs you nominate in software, which is why no two published diagrams agree and none of them is wrong. Leave six pins unconnected on a typical 14-pin module: SDO (MISO), since these drivers are write-only, and the five touch pins T_CLK, T_CS, T_DIN, T_DO and T_IRQ, which belong to a separate XPT2046 controller.

Buy a 40-way ribbon of jumpers rather than eight singles — about A$5, colour coded, and it peels apart. Pin 1 on the Pi has a square solder pad where every other pin is round; odd numbers run along one row and even along the other. There is a second 3.3 V pin at 17, so if you would rather the backlight were simply always on, run LED there instead of GPIO 18 — which is worth doing anyway if you ever want I²S audio, since GPIO 18 doubles as the PCM clock. None of these pins clash with the camera, which uses the CSI connector rather than GPIO.

If you cross-reference other guides, expect them to differ on those three: Adafruit puts DC on GPIO 18 and reset on 23, luma.lcd uses 23 and 24 with the backlight on 18, and the bare modules are usually documented as 25, 24 and 23. All are equally valid — just keep the wiring and the code in step.

Working examples to start from, roughly in order of usefulness here. python_ili9341 is a pure Python driver built around pushing arbitrary images with automatic partial updates — only the changed region goes down the wire, which is the single most useful property for a live feed. luma.examples holds runnable demos for the library recommended above, so it is the fastest route to a lit screen. MSSohan's 2.8″ interfacing repo is a concrete Pi build with its own wiring notes, handy as a second opinion, and Adafruit's user-space guide carries the matching code for their wiring page.

One genuinely different approach worth knowing about: fbcp-ili9341 copies the Pi's main framebuffer to the SPI panel, so it shows whatever the HDMI output shows. That would let this app keep running unmodified in its terminal and simply be mirrored to the small screen — no display code at all. The caveats are real: it is a C driver tuned for particular Pi models and OS versions, and it competes with the compositor for CPU. But if the goal is a second screen rather than a standalone build, it is the shortest path.

Three things catch people. Check VCC before powering up — some cheap boards carry a regulator and want 5 V while others are 3.3 V only, and getting that wrong destroys the board. The logic is 3.3 V either way, so unlike the STN panel above no level shifter is needed. Leave SDO/MISO and the five touch pins disconnected: these drivers are write-only, and touch is a separate XPT2046 controller you do not need. And enable SPI in raspi-config first, confirming /dev/spidev0.0 exists, before debugging anything else.

One caveat about the board itself: the plain Zero 2 W ships with an unpopulated GPIO header, so it needs pins fitted before any of this applies. The Zero 2 WH comes with them pre-soldered and is stocked in Australia by Core Electronics, Little Bird and Pi Australia. If your Zero already has pins on it, disregard this.

Sharp Memory LCD, 400 × 240

The largest character grid of any module here: 66×30, or 1,980 cells — more than a VT220's 80×24 screen holds. A 5:3 panel, so it keeps 81% of the frame, second only to the 320×240. SPI over three pins, 13.5 KB per frame, which the Pi will not notice.

It is reflective rather than emissive: no backlight, extraordinary contrast, daylight readable, and it looks like the display on a good calculator or a field instrument. Whether that reads as vintage is a judgement call — it is 1990s laboratory rather than 1980s home computer, and unlike everything else here it does not glow, which is why the sample below is drawn dark-on-light.

At 2.7″ it is about 172 ppi, so the characters are small. The practical advantage over everything above it: it is on a shelf in Newcastle at a known price, not a special order with a lead time.

Noritake vacuum fluorescent display

Ranked below both LCDs above, and losing to them on every measurable axis — fewer characters, more crop, three to six times the price, and a special order rather than shelf stock. It stays this high for one reason: it is the only thing here that glows, and that blue-green emission is the most distinctively period object on the page. If the look is the point, promote it.

Skip the character modules: the CU-Y series tops out around 20×4, or 80 characters total.

The usable parts are the graphic modules. GU256×128 gives about 42×16 characters at a 6×8 font. Note that this is a 2:1 grid, not 4:3 — a 6×8 cell is 1.33 times taller than it is wide, so 42×16 covers 252×128 pixels. Run fill mode and it crops the sensor to 2:1, keeping about two thirds of the frame height; run fit mode and the picture pillarboxes to 28×16 with blank columns either side.

Two further caveats: DigiKey lists it as not normally stocked, so expect a special order with a long lead time to Australia, and 42 columns is a coarse picture. Used GU256×64 modules turn up around A$60–150.

Two 128 × 128 OLEDs side by side

A pair of 1.5″ SSD1327 panels butted together gives 256×128 — the same 42×16 grid as the Noritake for about a fifth of the money. The Pi drives both without extra hardware: SPI0 has two hardware chip selects, so they appear as spidev0.0 and spidev0.1.

Bandwidth is a non-issue. At 4 bits per pixel each panel is 8,192 bytes per frame, so the pair needs 246 KB/s at 15 fps — against 1 MB/s from SPI at a conservative 8 MHz. Build the bitmap by pre-rendering the ten ramp glyphs once into a (10, 8, 6) array and indexing it, exactly as the app already maps brightness to characters — one vectorised operation, not a per-glyph draw.

The catch is the seam. Each active area is only about 27 mm wide, so 3–4 mm of combined bezel is 12–15% of a panel — the equivalent of 35–45 black pixels straight down the middle of the image, not politely at the edge. Better to use it than fight it: picture on the left panel, live status on the right, and the split reads as an instrument panel rather than a defect.

Cheapest retro panel

Amber OLED, 256 × 64

Available from Australian eBay sellers, Core Electronics in Newcastle and Little Bird. Amber-on-black reads as period-correct and it is the cheapest way to get a dedicated retro-looking panel. But this is a 4:1 grid, and that is punishing: fill mode keeps barely a third of the frame height, and fit mode pillarboxes the picture into just 14 of the 42 columns.

Stack two of them, though, and the arithmetic changes completely. Two 256×64 panels one above the other make 256×128 — the same 42×16 grid as the Noritake and the side-by-side pair, at A$60–120 for the pair. The frame retained goes from 34% back up to 68%.

The seam runs horizontally here, and for this content that is the better axis. A vertical join bisects every character row; a horizontal one leaves every row whole on one panel or the other, and interrupts the picture once rather than in all sixteen rows. The gap is shallower too — roughly three character rows against seven character widths — because the bezel along a module's long edge is narrower than the combined side bezels of two square panels. Mechanically it is easier as well: two identical-width modules butted long-edge to long-edge only need aligning on one axis.

128 × 64 OLED — the ubiquitous one

The 0.96″ SSD1306 module every hobby shop stocks, including Jaycar over the counter in Sydney. At a 6×8 font it is a 21×8 grid — the smallest thing on this page that still resolves into a picture, as the sample shows. Bandwidth is trivial: 1 bit per pixel means 1,024 bytes per frame, comfortable even on I²C at 400 kHz.

It has one genuine argument in its favour that the bigger panels lack. The SSD1306 is 1 bit per pixel with no greyscale at all, so character density is the only way to suggest tone — which makes ASCII the right technique here rather than a costume. Contrast that with the 128×128 SSD1327, whose 16 grey levels would look better fed the raw greyscale frame directly.

Physically it is tiny: ~149 ppi means a 6×8 cell is about 1.0 × 1.4 mm, so you read it with your nose near the glass. Best treated as a cheap proof of concept, or as the status half of a two-panel build.

The call

Get a local CRT now; wait for the terminal

Buy a local amber or green CRT off Gumtree or Marketplace for under A$100. It gives you authentic phosphor, full frame rate, and a grid big enough for a genuinely recognisable image — and if it turns out to be a dud you are out very little.

Then keep a saved search on eBay.com.au for a VT220, VT320 or VT510 and treat true character hardware as the upgrade when one appears at A$150–350.

If you would rather buy something today than wait for one to surface, the 320×240 graphic LCD at rank 3 is the pick: 53×30 characters, the whole field of view with no crop, letters big enough to read from a chair, and A$60–115. Go in knowing it is a build, not a wiring job — a parallel bus, 5 V level shifting, and a bias rail your module may or may not generate for itself. If that is not the weekend you had in mind, a 320×240 ILI9341 over SPI gives you the identical grid for about A$20 and five wires, on a smaller and more modern-looking screen.

Two things that do not change

The feedback loop does not need screenshots. For a character display, grim is the wrong tool anyway. The app already holds the exact character matrix in memory, so a --dump-frame option writing it to a text file gives the ground truth of what the display shows — better than a screenshot, and identical for a VFD, a terminal or a CRT. Mirroring to HDMI would be far more awkward, since only one process can open the camera.

None of these need code changes. The app fits its grid to whatever the terminal reports, which it demonstrated by tracking a live resize from 267×101 down to 123×50 without a stumble. Over serial, cell_metrics() returns nothing, so --cell-aspect governs — and its default of 2.0 is about right for a VT character cell.
