ASCII camera: from breadboard to enclosure

Taking the Pi, Camera Module 2, Waveshare 2.4″ ILI9341 panel and rotary encoder off the bench and into a self-contained, battery-powered box — with every solderless joint replaced by something that survives being carried around.

Where you are now

Two of your three connections are already sound. Only one is genuinely fragile, and it is the one holding the encoder.

One correction

The camera ribbon is not on an SPI connector — it plugs into the CSI-2 port, a dedicated camera interface. SPI lives on the GPIO header, which is where your panel is. Two separate buses that happen to sit near each other. Worth knowing before you search for replacement cables: “CSI cable” and “SPI cable” return very different products.

The breadboard is the part to be least sentimental about. Five jumpers through spring contacts is roughly ten mating surfaces held together by friction, and every one of them can go intermittent under vibration. An intermittent encoder does not look like a loose wire — it looks like a software bug, and you will spend an evening reading src/encoder.py before you suspect the breadboard.

What you end up with

Three boards sandwiched, three peripherals on latching harnesses, and one edge of the Pi left reachable. No enclosure in this view — this is the assembly you should be able to power up and test flat on the bench before anything gets printed.

The wires fan from two sockets, and the drawing spreads them for legibility — in reality each bundle stays together as one harness into one keyed plug. What matters here is the correspondence: read straight across and you have the wire to cut, the pad to solder, and the header pin it must reach.

Worth noting what is not in this picture: no breadboard, no loose jumpers, and no wire that terminates in anything other than a latching plug or a soldered joint. That is the whole point of the exercise.

Inside the HAT

This is the part you actually solder. Every wire in the drawing above stops at a JST-XH socket — here is what has to happen underneath it, from each outgoing socket position back to the header pin it belongs on.

Red for 3V3 and black for ground in both harnesses, because that convention is worth more than any drawing — anyone opening this box later will read those two correctly without a legend.

Two things fall out of this that are worth knowing before you start. The panel’s eight wires all land in the left half of the header (pins 1–24) and the encoder’s four all land in the right (pins 31–39), so the two sockets want to sit at opposite ends of the board and almost nothing has to cross. And the two grounds are separate pins — 6 for the panel, 39 for the encoder — which is worth keeping that way rather than daisy-chaining them, since it costs nothing and keeps the encoder’s switching current out of the panel’s return path.

Check every route with a multimeter in continuity mode before the HAT ever meets the Pi. A short between 3V3 and ground on a board you have just soldered is cheap to find now and expensive to find later.

Drop the encoder module

This is the largest simplification available, and your own code already permits it. src/encoder.py claims all three encoder pins with SET_PULL_UP, and the probe run confirmed that SW works on nothing but that internal pull-up — the module fits no resistor on it. The blue PCB’s only real contribution is the two 10 kΩ pull-ups on CLK and DT, which the Pi duplicates internally.

So replace it with a bare EC11 encoder. Three signal wires plus its common pin to ground. No PCB, no 3.3 V feed, and — the practical win — the EC11’s threaded bushing panel-mounts with a nut, where the round module PCB forces an oversized cutout and an awkward standoff behind the panel.

No code changes at all. Same three GPIOs, same driver.

A convenient accident

With the module gone, all four encoder conductors land on odd-numbered header pins at the far end of the connector: 31, 35, 37 and 39, with pin 39 already being a ground. One tidy cluster, one short harness.

Give it a tail board

Do not solder the harness straight to the encoder’s tags. A cable fixed at one end can only be fitted before the encoder goes into the panel, which forces you to assemble the box around the wiring — awkward, and worse every time you need to take it apart.

Instead solder a 4-way JST-XH header onto a ~15 mm square of perfboard, and solder that onto the EC11’s pins. The encoder becomes a self-contained module with a socket, so the cable plugs in at both ends. Mount the Pi, the panel and the encoder to the shell first, then drop the two harnesses in last.

It is worth naming what this is: approximately the breakout board you just removed — minus the pull-ups you do not need, and in a shape you choose rather than a round PCB that fights the panel nut. The connector was the module’s good idea; the resistors were the redundant part.

Connectors

Dupont fails in a portable build for one specific reason: the crimp has no latch and nothing but friction holding it in the housing, so vibration walks it out. Everything below is chosen to latch.

HarnessConnectorWhy this one
Panel, 8 wayJST-XH 2.5 mmMost forgiving to hand-crimp; latches; happy with 24–26 AWG
Encoder, 4 wayJST-XH 2.5 mmSame family again; header on a tail board so it unplugs at both ends
BatteryJST-PH 2.0 mmNot a choice; it is what the pack ships with
The cheapest possible fix, if you want one today

Your Waveshare cable already ends in nine individually crimped female Dupont shells. Those crimps have retention tabs and slide out of their single housings and into a multi-way housing with a pin pick — no crimper, no re-terminating. A 1×9 empty housing costs pennies and instantly removes the wrong-pin failure mode. It still will not latch to the header, so it is a stopgap rather than the destination, but it is twenty minutes of work.

Buy a real crimper. An IWISS SN-01BM or equivalent is the difference between connectors that last years and connectors you remake monthly. Crimping, not soldering, is the skill that decides whether this build stays reliable.

Why one family for both

It is tempting to fit the smaller JST-PH on the encoder because it is only four ways. Resist it. Mixing families means two sets of terminals, two housings, two die positions on the crimper and two things to keep in stock — real friction, for a one-off build, against very little gain. A 4-way XH is about 10 mm wide, which is nothing on a perfboard HAT.

The one honest argument for mixing is that different families cannot be cross-plugged. But the pin counts already do most of that work — an 8-way and a 4-way will not properly mate — and placement plus a paint-pen mark on pin 1 covers the rest.

Reach for PH on the encoder only if you wire it in 28 AWG ribbon, since XH terminals under-fill at that gauge where PH is specced to 30; or if you later move to a custom PCB, where 2.5 mm pitch genuinely costs layout area.

Ribbon or discrete wire?

The deciding factor is crimp fit, not electrical performance — at these lengths both carry the signals perfectly well.

Use 26 AWG silicone for both harnesses. XH terminals are sized for 22–26 AWG, so 26 AWG fills the barrel properly. Ribbon is typically 28 AWG, which under-fills and crimps weakly — the exact failure mode you are designing out.

If you prefer ribbon for tidiness — and it is tidier, with rainbow colouring making pin 1 unmistakable — then buy stranded, never solid-core. Solid-core IDC ribbon work-hardens and snaps at the first bend that sees movement. Take a 10-way for the panel’s eight signals and ground the two spare conductors at both ends, one either side of SCK; they then act as a return path for the fastest edge in the harness. Note that choosing ribbon is the case that pushes the encoder back to PH.

Silicone-insulated wire is the safer default wherever the harness moves. It stays flexible, shrugs off a soldering iron, and does not work-harden the way PVC hookup wire does after a few dozen flexes. That matters most if the panel ends up on a removable lid — in which case leave a service loop so opening the box does not tug the joint.

Heatshrink over every solder joint for strain relief, whichever you choose.

Measure before you order

Waveshare use more than one connector family across their panel range, and I cannot tell from here which is on yours. Put calipers on the pin pitch — 2.54 mm, 2.0 mm and 1.25 mm are all plausible — before buying mating parts. If it turns out to be 1.25 mm, keep the supplied cable and re-terminate only its Pi end.

Power

How long it runs depends far more on how you run it than on which pack you buy. A PiSugar 3’s 1200 mAh at 3.7 V is 4.44 Wh, of which roughly 3.9 Wh survives the boost to 5 V.

ConfigurationMeasured CPUEst. drawPiSugar 3
Terminal + panel, desktop up, WiFi on 96% of a core ~2.4 W ~1.6 h
Headless — --no-terminal, console boot, WiFi off 29% of a core ~1.45 W ~2.7 h

The CPU column is measured on this Pi, sampled from /proc over 15 s after a 35 s settle. The draw and runtime columns are estimates derived from it — a wattage needs a meter inline with the supply, which is worth doing once when the pack arrives.

A 1200 mAh PiSugar 3 is fine for a demo piece or a desk object, and it is meaningfully smaller and lighter than the 5000 mAh Plus — which matters for something handheld. If you want all-day untethered use, that is the case where you would want the Plus instead. Either way the PiSugar mounts on the back without consuming the GPIO header, so your HAT stays free, and brings charging, a power button and a real-time clock with it.

Recharging from flat takes roughly 2½–3 hours with the Pi shut down, and 4 hours or more if it is running from a small supply — your build draws ~600 mA, so a 1 A charger leaves only ~400 mA for the cell. Use a 2 A supply or better, and let the pack cool before charging: a battery straight out of a sealed enclosure is warm, and charging is throttled or inhibited until it drops back.

If you go with a plain USB bank instead, check it does not auto-shut-off below about 100 mA. At 600 mA you will not trip that in use, but some packs also refuse to start delivering power without a button press, which is awkward inside a sealed box.

Do this or you will corrupt an SD card

Pulling power from a running Pi is the failure you will actually hit in a sealed enclosure. Two defences, and I would fit both. First, a momentary button on a spare GPIO running a clean shutdown — the PiSugar’s own button does this out of the box. Second, and better: make the root filesystem read-only with an overlay (raspi-config → Performance → Overlay File System). Your app writes nothing but ascii_camera.log, which can live in tmpfs, so a power cut becomes an ordinary reboot and the box needs no shutdown ceremony at all.

Two ways to run it

Inside a box there is no HDMI screen, so --no-terminal stops being an optimisation and becomes the correct mode. Your own code says why it is cheap:

No terminal: the panel does its own downscale and character mapping on its own thread, so building a picture here would be work nothing ever looks at.

The terminal grid is 267 × 100 — 26,700 cells. The panel is 64 × 24 — 1,536 cells. Seventeen times less work per frame, and the measurement above bears it out: 96% of a core falls to 29%.

ModeCommandWhen
Enclosed --lcd --encoder --no-terminal On battery. Boot to console, no desktop, HDMI output off, WiFi off
Tethered fit --lcd --encoder On the bench with a monitor plugged in, for debugging

Make the enclosed mode the one that starts at boot, and opt into tethered mode deliberately. Anything that starts the terminal render automatically will quietly cost you 3.3× the CPU on a battery that cannot spare it.

Two further savings that cost you nothing in the box: --fps 10 eases both the camera and the render loop, and --lcd-brightness 60 takes roughly 40% off the backlight. Both are flags you already have.

Leaving a way in

Sealing the box is the moment debugging gets hard, so plan the access before you print rather than cutting it in afterwards.

One slot gets you everything

The Zero’s mini-HDMI and both micro-USB ports sit along the same long edge, opposite the GPIO header. A single rectangular opening down that edge exposes all three — no connectors, no extension cable, no signal-integrity question. Position the board against that wall and the cutout is the whole job.

That also buys two debugging routes you may reach for more often than a monitor:

  • USB gadget mode on the data port presents the Pi as an Ethernet device over one cable — SSH into a sealed box with no network at all.
  • The power port stays reachable for bench work without opening the case.

Bare slot or panel-mount socket?

ApproachEffortTrade-off
Slot for the whole port edgeTrivialNeeds a mini-HDMI lead; dust gets in
Panel-mount mini-HDMIModerateTidier; still needs a mini-HDMI lead
Panel-mount full-size HDMI + short internal adapterMostAny ordinary HDMI cable works

The real argument for a panel-mount socket is not neatness. The Zero’s mini-HDMI connector is surface-mounted and fragile, and repeated plugging stresses a joint on a board that will by then be wired into your HAT. A panel-mount socket takes that wear on a cheap replaceable part instead. If this is genuinely break-glass access, the bare slot is fine.

Two things that catch people

It is mini-HDMI (Type C) — not micro, not full size. Tape an adapter inside the lid or you will be hunting for one at the worst possible moment. And an open slot lets dust onto the board: a printed flap or a rubber bung costs nothing.

Consider a serial console too

A UART console on GPIO 14/15 is three wires to a 3-pin header, and it gives boot messages and a login shell when networking is dead — which is the situation HDMI usually gets reached for. It is far easier to expose than HDMI and catches early-boot output. It does not replace HDMI here, though: seeing the ASCII picture in terminal mode needs a real screen.

Build order

  1. Free the encoder

    Solder a 4-way JST-XH header onto a ~15 mm square of perfboard, then solder that tail board onto the EC11’s own pins: A, B, switch and common. Sleeve the joints in heatshrink.

    The encoder is now a module with a socket on it, which is the point — it can be screwed into the panel before any cable exists. The breadboard leaves the project permanently.

  2. Build the HAT

    Solder a 2×20 female header to a Zero-sized protoboard so it mates with the Pi’s male pins. On the top side, fit the two JST-XH sockets, 8-way for the panel and 4-way for the encoder, then wire each socket pin to the correct header pin per the tables above. Verify every route with a multimeter in continuity mode before the Pi ever goes near it.

  3. Re-terminate the panel cable

    Keep the Waveshare plug at the panel end. Cut the nine Dupont shells off the other end, drop the unused MISO conductor, and crimp the remaining eight into one JST-XH housing. Label pin 1 with a paint pen — you will thank yourself at reassembly.

  4. Test flat on the bench, before any enclosure

    Run bash run_ascii_camera.sh fit --lcd --encoder with everything loose on the desk. Confirm the picture, then turn the knob both ways and press it. Fixing a cold joint is trivial now and miserable once it is screwed into a box.

  5. Fit power and prove the shutdown path

    Add the battery, then deliberately test whatever shutdown you chose. If you went the overlay-filesystem route, yank the power mid-run on purpose and confirm it boots cleanly. Better to discover that now than in the field.

  6. Model and print the enclosure

    Measure your own modules — panel PCBs vary between vendors, typically around 50 × 70 mm with a 49 × 37 mm active area. Cut the window to the active area, not the board outline, or you will frame the bezel. Print in PETG: PLA softens near 55 °C and a sealed box in Sydney sun gets there.

    Cut the port-edge slot now, in the model — see Leaving a way in. Retrofitting an opening for mini-HDMI into a printed shell is fiddly and never looks deliberate.

  7. Final assembly and strain relief

    Panel on M2 standoffs behind its window. EC11 through a single hole, nut outside, with a small anti-rotation notch or the whole encoder turns when the knob stiffens. Clamp the camera ribbon to the shell about a centimetre back from its connector so flexing happens in the free span, never at the joint. Fit all three devices to the shell first, then plug the two harnesses in — that is what the connectors at both ends of each cable buy you.

Three things that will bite

SPI at 40 MHz dislikes long wire

Keep MOSI and SCK under about 10 cm. If you went the ribbon route, the grounded spare conductors either side of SCK are doing this job for you. You already have the escape hatch if it misbehaves: --lcd-spi-hz 20000000 halves the clock, and your README documents exactly that.

Re-crimping can reverse the knob

Which rotation counts as forwards depends on which physical pin lands on GPIO 19. Swap A and B while making the new harness and the knob runs backwards. The fix is --encoder-reverse, not a rewire — so test it before you blame the crimp.

The Zero 2 throttles, and your render loop is not gentle

Vents above and below the board for convection, plus a stick-on heatsink. A sealed box with no airflow will quietly cost you frame rate long before it produces an error message.
